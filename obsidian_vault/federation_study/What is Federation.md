# What is Federation

> Source basis: Ter-Minassian (ed.) 1997, IMF; Rondinelli in Litvack & Seddon (eds.) 1999, World Bank Institute; Rondinelli, Nellis & Cheema 1983, World Bank; Litvack, Ahmad & Bird 1998, World Bank; Faguet 2021, LSE; Blume & Voigt 2008; Papagianni 2006; Schragger 2010; Smoke 2001, UNRISD; CeFiMS, Univ. of London; Oates 1999; Boex, Williamson & Yilmaz 2021, World Bank; Stein & Turkewitsch 2008; Buchanan 1952; Bucovetsky 1998; Kant 1795. See [[corpus_profile]].

**Federation** is a state model in which sovereignty is **shared** between a general (federal) government and constituent regional governments, each exercising constitutionally guaranteed authority within its own sphere. It sits between the unitary state (center holds all sovereignty, concedes some to units) and the confederation (states retain sovereignty, delegate a minimum to a joint body).

## The three state models

| Model | Where sovereignty lives | Autonomy vs. unity |
|---|---|---|
| **Unitary state** | Central government holds all sovereignty; concedes a share to subordinate units | Autonomy subordinated to unity |
| **Federation** | Constitutionally divided between federal and regional tiers; regional units represented in federal institutions | Autonomy **and** unity balanced |
| **Confederation** | Member states retain sovereignty; joint body has only delegated powers | Unity subordinated to autonomy |

## Why federations form (the economic logic)

Federation has a rational economic core. **Bucovetsky (1998)** models federation as an insurance arrangement: when regions are uncertain about future resource rents, differ in expected rents, and poplations are risk-averse, a federation with limited redistribution is chosen because it lets residents of poor regions share in rich regions' rents **through migration** rather than pure transfers:

> "federation may be chosen by a group of countries, even though it is a more wasteful arrangement than either other alternative, if asymmetries among countries, and the degree of risk aversion, are large. Federal states also often implement transfers among the regions. These transfers can be viewed as substitutes for fiscal migration."

**Buchanan (1952)** adds the classical framing: grants between tiers exist because factor-price equalization through free trade and migration does **not** fully equalize incomes — transfers finish the job markets leave unfinished.

## Federation vs. decentralization : the crucial cut

A common error is treating "federation" and "decentralization" as the same thing. They operate on **different levels**. **Blume & Voigt (2008)** which explained the important distinctions

> "Both federal as well as unitary states can decentralize or centralize some activities. But in unitary states, the decision to decentralize can be revoked if the central government so wishes. This shows that even after decentralization, ultimate decision making power remains with the central government level. The two terms federation and decentralization deal, hence, with different levels of decision making: Federation refers to a trait in the constitutional level whereas the term decentralization describes a policy choice on the post-constitutional one."

**Federation is a constitutional trait; decentralization is a policy choice.** A federal structure can *host* decentralization policy. Conversely, a federal constitution does not by itself deliver decentralization.

The Spain/Germany contrast (CeFiMS, following Dafflon & Madiès 2009) proves the two variables are independent: **Spain is unitary yet highly decentralized; Germany is federal yet centralizing.** State model and degree of decentralization do not correlate.

**Papagianni (2006)** sharpens the separation, federation combines self-rule with shared rule at the center:

> "federalism combines self-rule by regions with shared rule at the centre," whereas autonomy "is a special arrangement between the centre and one or two regions... Autonomy models do not include Upper Houses."

And **Schragger (2010)**, following Feeley & Rubin, draws the line through the concept of power itself:

> "federalism is a trump wielded by local units against central units whether or not local units are advancing the benefits of administrative decentralization."

**Why this matters for an archipelagic adopter:** choosing a federal constitution fixes the *rules of the game* (who can revoke what); The Indonesia case in [[How Federation Resources Planning Affected Archipelagos Nation]] demonstrates exactly this. A formally unitary state running deeper real world decentralization than many federations, via asymmetric special status.

## What decentralization actually is (canonical typology)

The working definition across the World Bank lineage  **Rondinelli, Nellis & Cheema (1983)**, the typology's origin:

> "Decentralization can be defined as the transfer of responsibility for planning, management and resource raising and allocation from the central government and its agencies to: (a) field units of central government ministries or agencies, (b) subordinate units or levels of government, (c) semiautonomous public authorities or corporations, (d) areawide, regional or functional authorities, or (e) nongovernmental private or voluntary organizations"

**Rondinelli in Litvack & Seddon (1999)** gives the four-type umbrella:

> "Decentralization the transfer of authority and responsibility for public functions from the central government to subordinate or quasi independent government organizations or the private sector-covers a broad range of concepts. Each type of decentralization political, administrative, fiscal, and market has different characteristics, policy implications, and conditions for success."

### The four types

**Political decentralization** : power to citizens and their representatives:

> "Political decentralization aims to give citizens and their elected representatives more power in public decisionmaking. It is often associated with pluralistic politics and representative government, but it can also support democratization by giving citizens or their representatives more influence in formulating and implementing policies." (Rondinelli in Litvack & Seddon 1999)

**Administrative decentralization** : three forms, ascending strength:

| Form | Definition | What stays central |
|---|---|---|
| **Deconcentration** | "the redistribution of decisionmaking authority and financial and management responsibilities among different levels of the central government" | Everything — field units remain inside central government |
| **Delegation** | transfer to "semi-autonomous organizations not wholly controlled by the central government, but ultimately accountable to it" | Ultimate responsibility; a principal-agent relation (Litvack, Ahmad & Bird 1998) |
| **Devolution** | "the transfer of authority for decisionmaking, finance, and management to quasi-autonomous units of local government with corporate status" | Only indirect, supervisory control |

On devolution's specifics, Rondinelli in Litvack & Seddon (1999): devolved governments "elect their own mayors and councils, raise their own revenues, and have independent authority to make investment decisions." Rondinelli, Nellis & Cheema (1983) adds the legal criterion: local units are "autonomous and independent... their legal status makes them separate or distinct from the central government."

Litvack, Ahmad & Bird (1998) add the sharp edge: **deconcentration may not even be decentralization** 

> "The 'decentralization' that has occurred in many unitary countries is actually deconcentration, since independent local governments (which are legally accountable to local constituents) do not exist and local field offices of the central government are simply used to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of service delivery."

**Fiscal decentralization** : the money side. CeFiMS states the four pillars, which are exactly the links of the resource-planning chain in [[What is Resources Planning]]:

> "There is broad consensus in the literature that fiscal decentralisation has four pillars: expenditure assignments; revenue assignments, including tax and non-tax revenues as well as revenue sharing arrangements; transfer mechanisms; local borrowing."

And its domain boundary: "fiscal decentralisation only concerns subnational levels of government. Other local entities such as de-concentrated agencies or parastatals are excluded". The key difference from administrative decentralization.

Rondinelli in Litvack & Seddon (1999) lists its instruments: user charges, co-financing, local property/sales taxes, intergovernmental transfers, and municipal borrowing.

**Market decentralization** : privatization plus deregulation:

> "The most complete forms of decentralization from a government's perspective are privatization and deregulation; they shift responsibility for functions from the public to the private sector." (Rondinelli in Litvack & Seddon 1999)

### The definitions are contested (and knowing that is part of the definition)

The typology is useful but not settled. Three cautions this vault adopts:

**1. The types overlap.** Rondinelli himself concedes:

> "these concepts overlap considerably. Political, administrative, fiscal, and market decentralization can appear in different forms and combinations across countries, within countries, and even within sectors. Precise definitions are less important than ensuring a comprehensive approach." (Litvack & Seddon 1999)

**2. Pooling the forms is a methodological error.** **Faguet (2021)** delivers the sharpest critique in the literature:

> "deconcentration, delegation, devolution, and privatization differ fundamentally in organizational terms. They establish incentives that are fundamentally different from one another, which public officials, being rational respond to in fundamentally different ways. We should not expect their effects to be similar, and indeed they are not. Studies that compare countries that deconcentrated with others that devolved or delegated are committing a basic methodological error."

Faguet also demotes privatization from the typology ("it should not, in my view, be classed as a form of decentralization"), a position CeFiMS reports as a live dispute: "For [purists], decentralisation is strictly a state reform and concerns the public sector only."

**3. Fiscal-decentralization *measurement* is contested.** **Smoke (2001)** attacks the standard proxy:

> "fiscal decentralization is typically defined as the subnational share of total government expenditure... it problematically abstracts from political and institutional context."

His Kenya/Indonesia inversion proves it: the models would rank Indonesia more fiscally decentralized while the substantive attributes favor Kenya.

## Multi-level governance as the successor concept

**Stein & Turkewitsch (2008)** show that federalism theory and multi-level governance (MLG) evolved together : the causal arrow runs both ways. Federalism supplied MLG's early analytical categories; MLG now covers coordination problems (EU, networks) that classical federalism theory did not.

## Decentralization's economic rationale

**Oates (1999)** states the principle behind the whole structure:

> "the basic principle of fiscal decentralization: the presumption that the provision of public services should be located at the lowest level of government encompassing, in a spatial sense, the relevant benefits and costs."

This "Decentralization Theorem" is the normative bridge between the state models above and the resource-planning mechanics in the rest of this vault.

## The fiscal side defines a federation in practice

A federation is fiscal before it is symbolic. **Boex, Williamson & Ylmaz (2021)** show that what distinguishes real federations from nominal ones is the intergovernmental architecture: which tier raises revenue, which spends, and what transfers bridge the gap. A constitution can declare a federal structure, but if all revenue is centrally raised and passed down as discretionary grants, the system behaves unitary.

**Kant (1795)** supplies the philosophical origin: a "federation of free states" as a lawful substitute for the state of war among states — the earliest argument that federation is a *constitution-governed* order rather than a power bargain.
