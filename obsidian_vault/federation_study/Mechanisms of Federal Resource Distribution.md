# Mechanisms of Federal Resource Distribution

> Source basis: Ter-Minassian 1997, IMF; Al-Samarrai & Lewis 2021, World Bank; Haque et al. 2026, World Bank; Seidel & Schrooten 2000; OECD 2013; Hutchinson & LaFond 2004. See [[corpus_profile]].

Federal systems move money between tiers through four main mechanisms. The **design details** — not the choice of mechanism — decide whether the result is equalization or perverse redistribution.

## The four mechanisms

### 1. Revenue sharing (tax sharing)

A tax base is shared between tiers by rule. Design choices:
- **Derivation-based** — revenue returns to the region where it was collected. Regressive: rich regions keep more.
- **Formula-based** — shared revenue is apportioned by redistributive criteria.

**Ter-Minassian (1997)** on the range of practice:

> "in Germany shared revenue from the VAT is apportioned on a per capita basis, which entails a degree of redistribution to the less affluent states. India utilizes formulas that combine population, income per capita, indicators of backwardness, and the state's own tax effort."

The German and Indian examples show the two design poles: per capita (simple, mildly redistributive) vs. multi-indicator (targets backwardness explicitly, rewards tax effort).

### 2. Grants (transfers)

- **General-purpose / unconditional** — no earmarking; supports the recipient's overall budget
- **Conditional / specific** — earmarked for a sector or project
- **Matching** — requires the recipient to co-finance

Ter-Minassian's reform guideline is the architecture's core preference:

> "open-ended, matching, and conditional grants, which inherently entail significant uncertainty for both donor and recipient governments, are clearly inferior to formula-based general purpose grants."

Predictability beats precision: a simple stable formula outperforms a sophisticated volatile one.

### 3. Equalization transfers

The explicit redistributive instrument. Four design traditions (Ter-Minassian 1997):

| Country | Equalization basis | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Australia** | Full equalization — "equal ability of the states to provide a standard level of public services at an average level of taxation" | "the most comprehensive effort at equalization, but it is also very complex, and demanding in terms of information requirements" |
| **Canada** | Relative **taxing capacities** only; expenditure needs excluded | Simple, but ignores demand-side differences |
| **Denmark, Japan, Korea** | Simple expenditure-need indicators — demographics, population density, road length | Low data burden; coarse targeting |
| **Germany** | VAT per capita apportionment | Transparent; limited redistribution |

### 4. Asymmetric special status

Some regions get special fiscal regimes — usually for political, geographic, or resource reasons. Indonesia's Papua receives 80% of forestry, fishery and mining revenue and 70% of oil and gas income (Simandjuntak 2018). This mechanism is powerful but the least disciplined: see [[Systemic Risks & Failure Modes in Developing Federations]].

## Formula design: what actually determines performance

**Al-Samarrai & Lewis (2021)** document what formulas do wrong in practice:

**Perverted weights (Indonesia DAU).** The general transfer formula equalizes across *jurisdictions*, not *people*:

> "The allocation formula for these transfers is designed to equalize funding among subnational units but not among individuals. This results in districts with large populations receiving lower per capita transfers than districts with smaller populations."

**No cost adjustment.** Very few formulas adjust for cost-of-provision differences:

> "Costs diverge among regions because the prices of key inputs vary (for example, the cost of delivering textbooks to remote mountainous regions is higher than that for urban schools) ... Poorer municipalities often incur high costs in providing education services and are disadvantaged further if transfer mechanisms fail to take this into account."

**Perverse incentives (Indonesia teacher salaries).** The formula's salary incentive distorted hiring:

> "the formula for the largest general transfers includes incentives for district governments to spend more on hiring civil service teachers than on nonsalary spending ... salary spending accounts for approximately three-quarters of subnational education spending."

**Formula that punishes poverty (Sudan FFAMC).** The eight-criteria formula rewarded the already-strong:

> "states that are able to generate more own-revenue and those that are able to carry a budget surplus are favored in the allocation formula, which defeats the underlying objective of achieving greater equity across states."

Plus: "There is little to no evidence that this formula is being applied."

**Simplicity as a design rule.** **Hutchinson & LaFond (2004)**: Philippines IRA — 50% population, 25% land area, 25% category — is simple, but "may bear little relation to differences in need or in the costs of service provision." Uganda's block grants (population + infant mortality) get closer to need. **Haque et al. (2026, Box 11)** set the discipline explicitly:

> "The design of a fiscal equalization program for Pakistan must be simple, objective, transparent, easy to understand, and based on publicly available data verified by federal and provincial authorities. Any official or private citizen must be able to easily duplicate calculations."

## The institutional wrapper

Mechanisms fail without institutions around them. **Seidel & Schrooten (2000)**:

> "to take advantage of the potential efficiency gains offered by fiscal federalism, the vertical co-ordination of revenue and expenditure responsibilities between the different governmental levels should be clarified. Problems of destruction and enforcement should be eliminated and replaced by harmonization and co-operation."

**OECD (2013)** adds the coordination layer: conditionalities must be "evidence-based, observable, allowing for process and ex post verification"; contracts across tiers must be "not only credible, but verifiable commitments."
