# How Geographical Problems Pushed For Federation

> Source basis: Bucovetsky 1998; Simandjuntak 2018; Anisyah et al. 2026; Said et al. 2020; Ortiz et al. 2023. See [[corpus_profile]].

Geography is not a background variable in fiscal design — it is a **first-order cause** of why multi-tier arrangements get chosen, and of which tier ends up carrying which burden.

## The theoretical root: asymmetric regions create the demand for federation

**Bucovetsky (1998)** models federation as a response to asymmetry. Federation becomes attractive when regions differ in expected resource rents and populations are risk-averse — the arrangement lets risk-sharing substitute for pure migration:

> "what will be required for federalism to be chosen in the model developed here are: (i) uncertainty about resource rents (ii) differences among regions (ex ante) in their expected resource rents (iii) risk aversion."

The mechanism: poor-region residents can share rich-region rents *by moving there*, but migration wastes resources. Transfers do the same job with less waste. **Geographic asymmetry is the reason the insurance contract exists.**

## The archipelagic mechanism: water fragmentation

In archipelagic nations, geography does not merely create regional asymmetry — it creates a distinct, measurable fiscal penalty. **Anisyah et al. (2026)** name the causal chain:

> "Compared with mainland regions, archipelagic provinces face distinctive fiscal challenges arising from dispersed populations, fragmented territories, higher transportation and logistics costs, uneven economic agglomeration, and higher public service delivery costs. These structural characteristics may constrain local governments' ability to generate own-source revenues while simultaneously increasing expenditure needs."

Two-sided squeeze: **revenue side** (limited agglomeration → weak local tax base) and **expenditure side** (fragmented service delivery → higher unit costs).

## The Indonesian evidence: when the formula ignores geography

**Simandjuntak (2018)** documents the precise mechanism by which archipelagic geography becomes fiscal disadvantage — the **DAU** (general-purpose grant) formula:

> "The DAU formula only considers variables such as the size of land areas and of the population. With this formula, archipelagic provinces have been receiving less transfers than terrestrial provinces."

The eight archipelagic provinces are defined by water share:

| Province | Water share of total area |
|---|---|
| Kepri | 96% |
| Maluku | 93% |
| North Maluku | 91% |
| East Nusa Tenggara | 88% |
| West Nusa Tenggara | 85% |
| North Sulawesi | 79% |
| Bangka-Belitung | 76% |
| Southeast Sulawesi | 72% |

Population counts against them too — Kepri is 251,810 km² but only ~2 million inhabitants across 2,408 islands. A formula weighting land and population systematically under-weights water area and dispersion.

**Counterintuitive result:** adding waters to the formula does not fix it cleanly. Simandjuntak reports the 2012 simulation findings — raising the water weight from 30% toward 100% boosts archipelagic DAU **but** (a) does not significantly raise provincial growth, and (b) above 80% weight "would not result in equitable financial capacity, as the increase of DAU of archipelagic regions would reduce those of non-archipelagic provinces." Equalization is zero-sum in a fixed pool.

## The local pattern: resources accumulate on the mainland

**Said et al. (2020)** show the micro-level consequence — within a single archipelagic regency (Sumenep):

> "The archipelagic area is often left behind from the mainland in terms of development conducted by the local government. It happens because generally, the mainland is the center of government and the economy; therefore, the distribution of resources tends to accumulate in the mainland."

Budget allocations to mainland and islands **negate each other** — "affirmations in one area result in weaker allocations in other areas." See [[Tradeoff With The Federation Resources Planning]] for the trade-off structure.

## Island jurisdictions as a governance category

**Ortiz et al. (2023)** supply the typology that generalizes the argument beyond Indonesia — SIDS (sovereign), **subnational island jurisdictions (SNIJ)** (semi-autonomous islands with real legislative competence), and dependent islands. SNIJs face the same squeeze as Indonesia's archipelagic provinces: real autonomy, weak fiscal base, dependence on central transfers.

## Why geography pushes toward federation (or special status)

The chain this vault note documents:

1. Geographic fragmentation (islands, mountains, remoteness) creates heterogeneous **expenditure needs**
2. Fragmentation also creates heterogeneous **revenue capacity**
3. A uniform unitary formula cannot represent this heterogeneity
4. Distinct regions demand either **asymmetric special status** (Indonesia's archipelagic bill; Papua, Aceh) or a **federal structure** with constitutionally distinct powers
5. Federation + geography-matched transfers is the theoretical optimum; special-status unitary is the empirical compromise
