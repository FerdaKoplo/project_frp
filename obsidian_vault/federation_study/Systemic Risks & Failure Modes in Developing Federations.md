# Systemic Risks & Failure Modes in Developing Federations

> Source basis: Moges 2003 (Ethiopia); Haque et al. 2026 (Pakistan); Chaitkin 2022 (Nigeria); Arends 2020 (danger taxonomy); Al-Samarrai & Lewis 2021 (Sudan). See [[corpus_profile]].

Five case-documented failure modes. Each is **structural**, not accidental — the political economy produces them. An archipelagic federation adopting resource planning should design against all five.

## Failure Mode 1: Decentralization without capacity → big, weak government

**Moges (2003), Ethiopia:**

> "when fiscal decentralization is exercised with high horizontal and vertical imbalances, it fails to diversify public output in line with the preferences and priorities of local population and to internalize the decisions of regional governments within their own jurisdictions. This in turn encourages the prevalence of big and yet weak government that extracts resources and fails to allocate for the purpose of sustainable and shared economic growth."

Driver: vertical imbalance without commensurate revenue devolution:

> "Given the prevailing vertical imbalance, the system has not internalized the cost of their expenditure decisions by regions. This in turn tends to give incentive to expansionary fiscal policy stance and bigger government."

**Failure condition:** spending authority devolved, revenue authority withheld. Regions spend without bearing the cost.

## Failure Mode 2: Unpredictable funding → disorderly resource allocation

**Haque et al. (2026), Pakistan §5.2.1:**

> "Provincial PEFA assessments note weak predictability of transfers and in-year resource allocation, reliance on quarterly or partial releases and centralized cash management, and limited advance communication of ceilings to lower levels. When funding flows are unpredictable, service delivery units cannot plan procurement, maintenance, or staffing effectively."

Structural driver — volatility is pushed **down** the chain:

> "the burden of adjustment is therefore pushed downward through the PFM chain... schools, health facilities, and district administrations operate in an environment characterized by interrupted cash flows and the absence of credible medium-term expenditure frameworks."

**Failure condition:** aggregate fiscal control retained at the top, adjustment cost absorbed at the bottom.

## Failure Mode 3: Formulas that reward the strong (equalization inverts)

**Al-Samarrai & Lewis (2021), Sudan:**

> "states that are able to generate more own-revenue and those that are able to carry a budget surplus are favored in the allocation formula, which defeats the underlying objective of achieving greater equity across states."

> "There is little to no evidence that this formula is being applied. The complexity of the formula has made it difficult for the federal government to clearly communicate the basis on which it decides the amount of federal resources to be transferred to the states."

Pakistan's NFC framework shows the same inversion: "The horizontal distribution formula also does not achieve genuine fiscal equalization... provides no meaningful incentives for provincial revenue effort or service delivery performance."

**Failure condition:** complexity → opacity → capture. Complex formulas are not just hard to administer — they are easier to subvert.

## Failure Mode 4: Fragmentation and intergovernmental rivalry

**Chaitkin (2022), Nigeria — vertical fragmentation:**

> "The fiscal-federalism design concentrates revenue at the center while assigning spending to the states and localities. Nigeria states jealously guard their fiscal autonomy and their planning, management, and implementation of government spending is largely disconnected from corresponding processes at the federal level."

The center raises ~90% of revenue; states and LGAs do most of the spending, and states erode the local tier's share: "diverting federal revenue intended for LGAs to state-defined priorities and operating parallel revenue boards."

Pakistan's version is tax-base fragmentation — the 18th Amendment "split the tax base between five competing jurisdictions. The resulting complexity imposes high compliance costs, discourages interprovincial trade, and has constrained aggregate revenue performance."

**Failure condition:** revenue and expenditure assignments misaligned across multiple tiers → coordination breakdown → each tier optimizes locally.

## Failure Mode 5: Transfer dependence crowds out local effort

**Anisyah et al. (2026), Indonesia**: provinces with weak own-source revenue remain transfer-dependent; the cycle reinforces itself.

**Simandjuntak (2018)**, on Indonesia's proposed archipelagic bill: "increasing central transfers will also perpetuate the dependence of the regions on central government resources."

**Failure condition:** transfers fund near-100% of subnational spending → no local tax effort → no accountability feedback.

## The underlying driver: political control beats planning rules

The clearest single explanation, from Pakistan's political-economy analysis (Haque et al. 2026, Box 9):

> "higher levels of government have strong reasons to retain control over fiscal resources, because control over money enables the mobilization and maintenance of political support. Development funds, transfers, and staffing decisions are powerful tools in managing coalitions, rewarding constituencies, and responding to political pressures. In this context, predictability, strict rule-based allocation, and strong oversight can reduce discretion, contradicting political imperatives."

**Transfer design is never politically neutral.** Every failure mode above is what rule-based planning looks like when discretion reasserts itself.

## The taxonomy of decentralization dangers

**Arends (2020)** organizes the failure space via Musgrave's three objectives — stability, efficiency, equity — plus accountability and corruption. His synthesis:

> "the dangers of decentralization are highly relevant to local public services in terms of the efficiency, equality, and accountability of service provision. Although there is evidence to support both the decentralization-enthusiastic and the decentralization-skeptical views, there are means of mitigating risks: When decentralizing public services, reformers should know the specificities of the respective public service, the local context, and the effects of the design of fiscal relations like the backs of their hands. If things go wrong, recentralization is a viable option."

## Design safeguards (literature-supported)

| Failure mode                 | Safeguard                                                                                                                  |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| F1 capacity gap              | Sequence authority after capacity; build tax administration first (Moges, Anisyah)                                         |
| F2 unpredictability          | Formula-based general grants over ad hoc conditional grants (Ter-Minassian); multi-year ceilings enforced (Haque)          |
| F3 captured/inverted formula | Simplicity + replicability: "Any official or private citizen must be able to easily duplicate calculations" (Haque Box 11) |
| F4 fragmentation             | Clear constitutional assignment; contracts as "verifiable commitments" (OECD 2013)                                         |
| F5 dependence                | Cap transfer share of subnational budget; reward own-revenue effort in formula (Ter-Minassian; Simandjuntak)               |
