# What is Resources Planning

> Source basis: Ter-Minassian 1997, IMF; OECD 2013; Boex, Williamson & Yilmaz 2021; Park 2024; Hutchinson & LaFond 2004. See [[corpus_profile]].

**Resource planning** in a multi-level government system is the deliberate alignment of **who raises money, who spends it, how the gap between the two is bridged, and how execution is disciplined** so that subnational governments can actually deliver services. It is not a single document or a single formula — it is a chain of five linked decisions, and it fails link by link, not all at once.

## The five-link chain

| # | Link | Core question | What it decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Expenditure assignment** | Which tier spends on what? | Which level is responsible for each public service |
| 2 | **Revenue assignment** | Which tier taxes what? | Which tax bases each level may tap |
| 3 | **Intergovernmental transfers** | How is the gap bridged? | Vertical gap (own revenue < own spending) + horizontal equalization (rich vs. poor regions) |
| 4 | **Budgeting / PFM discipline** | Can subnational actors actually plan? | Predictability of in-year resources, performance linkage, commitment controls |
| 5 | **Multi-year investment planning** | Is today's spending part of a strategy? | Linking strategic plans to multi-annual budgets across all tiers |

## Link 1–2: the assignment principle

**Ter-Minassian (1997, IMF)** states the classical rule:

> "Efficiency in the allocation of resources is best served by assigning responsibility for each type of public expenditure to the level of government that most closely represents the beneficiaries of these outlays."

Applied:
- **Central/federal** — defense, foreign affairs, interstate infrastructure, social insurance (risk-pooling), stabilization-sensitive spending (e.g., unemployment benefits)
- **State/province** — regional infrastructure (roads), shared agriculture/environment
- **Local** — police, fire, sanitation, utilities, primary and secondary education

Revenue assignment mirrors it. The center gets taxes with mobile bases, high income elasticity, and uneven regional distribution — corporate income tax, natural-resource rents, trade taxes. Local governments get immobile bases — property taxes, business licenses, user fees. Separating spending authority from revenue responsibility "obscure[s] the link between the benefits of public expenditures and their price" and destroys fiscal discipline.

## Link 3: transfers as the bridge

Assignments are never perfectly balanced — that imbalance is by design. The transfer system has two jobs: close the **vertical gap** (tier X must spend more than it raises) and narrow the **horizontal gap** (poor jurisdictions cannot fund the same services as rich ones).

## Link 4: predictability is the invisible link

The least-visible but most-felt link. **Haque et al. (2026, World Bank, Pakistan)** document what happens when it breaks:

> "When funding flows are unpredictable, service delivery units cannot plan procurement, maintenance, or staffing effectively. This leads to reactive management, interruptions in service provision, and an erosion of accountability for results, even where budgets have been formally approved." (§5.2.1, "Disorderly Resource Allocation")

## Link 5: multi-year planning across tiers

**OECD (2013)** sets the positive template: subnational governments need the capacity to link strategic plans to multi-annual budgets. Its 15-capacity framework groups the requirements into five goals — design a sound investment portfolio; ensure adequate resources; ensure accountability; promote results and learning; support all stages of the investment cycle.

## Why "planning" rather than "allocation"

Single-shot allocation decisions (this year's budget) are not planning. **Park (2024)** documents how performance information fails to discipline allocation in practice; **Hutchinson & LaFond (2004)** note the same failure in health sectors:

> "Decentralization is intended to improve the allocation of resources to priority health areas by making use of the informational advantages that local planners may have over centralized health officials. The results on the ground, however, frequently tell a very different story."

Planning means **rules that survive turnover in officials** — formula-based, multi-year, and verifiable. A transfer received by lobbying is an allocation; a transfer received by formula is a plan.

## Optimization framing

Resource planning can be stated as a constrained problem:

- **Objective** — equalize service-delivery capacity across tiers and regions, subject to efficiency
- **Constraints** — total revenue envelope; vertical balance; inflation/macro stability; political acceptability (formulas legislated, not discretionary); data availability (formula inputs must be measurable)
- **Instruments** — expenditure assignments, revenue assignments, transfer formulas (base, weights, conditionalities), budget rules (deficit/borrowing limits), asymmetric special status
