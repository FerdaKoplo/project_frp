# How Federation Resources Planning Affected Archipelagos Nation

> Source basis: Simandjuntak 2018; Anisyah et al. 2026; Said et al. 2020; Rijoly et al. 2026; Dwipatna et al. 2026; Al-Samarrai & Lewis 2021 (Indonesia chapters). See [[corpus_profile]].

**Evidence-base honesty note:** the corpus's archipelagic evidence is essentially Indonesia-only. Indonesia is formally **unitary** with asymmetric decentralization (special status for Aceh, Papua, Jakarta, Yogyakarta) — not a federation. This note therefore reports what Indonesia's intergovernmental resource planning actually does to archipelagic provinces, and extracts design transferable to an archipelagic federation.

## The core finding: planning widens the very gaps it targets

Indonesia has run systematic intergovernmental transfers since 2001 and reformed them under **Law No. 1 of 2022 (UU HKPD)**. Results across the archipelagic provinces:

**Anisyah et al. (2026)** — 8 archipelagic provinces, 2023–2025, HKPD's first years:

> "fiscal decentralization continues to produce unequal fiscal capacity among archipelagic provinces, supporting Fiscal Federalism Theory by showing fiscal autonomy and transfer dependency as complementary dimensions, with transfers functioning as equalization mechanisms and geographical context shaping these disparities."

Provinces with stronger own-source revenue (PAD) show higher fiscal autonomy and lower transfer dependency; the coefficient of variation across provinces declined over time but "substantial autonomy gaps persist."

> "The geographical condition of a scattered archipelago results in higher public service costs, while economic activity is relatively concentrated in certain areas, thereby further limiting local fiscal space. Thus, the increase in fiscal authority granted through the HKPD Law has not automatically led to an increase in fiscal capacity if it is not supported by regional economic growth and the optimization of local revenue potential."

**Lesson: authority ≠ capacity.** Granting fiscal authority does not create the tax base or lower the service-delivery cost.

## The DAU formula: the mechanism of mainland bias

**Simandjuntak (2018)**: the general-purpose grant (DAU) formula weighted **land area and population** — both systematically favor terrestrial provinces. The 2014 regional-government law added provincial waters to the formula, but the increase was too weak to change outcomes. Eight provinces (72–96% water) then pushed the **archipelagic-regions bill** (deliberated 2018) for special status and additional transfers.

Three structural findings from that episode:

1. **Formula weights are distributive politics.** "increasing DAU based on territorial waters will not reduce fiscal inequalities across regions, as the increase of transfers to archipelagic regions will decrease those to non-archipelagic regions." Fixed pool: helping islands taxes the mainland.
2. **Dependence is the price of transfers.** "increasing central transfers will also perpetuate the dependence of the regions on central government resources."
3. **Special status without capacity does not deliver.** Papua's asymmetric deal (80% of forestry/fishery/mining revenue, 70% of oil and gas until 2026) — "the special treatment has not significantly improved local welfare. Papua's poverty rate since 2014 stays stagnant at around 28%, the highest in Indonesia."

## The local-level trade-off: mainland vs. islands within one regency

**Said et al. (2020)**, Sumenep Regency (Madura archipelago):

> "Local government budgets behave in trade-offs, between the mainland and the islands area negating each other in their allocations. Affirmations in one area result in weaker allocations in other areas. The dilemma is that local governments are committed to eliminating regional disparities but are not supported by adequate financial capacity."

The systems-archetype diagnosis: **"success to the successful"** — "If an entity leads, then the entity will easier to continue to lead and improve its performance, while other entities remain underdeveloped." Budget trade-offs, limited financial capacity, and regional disparities form one reinforcing loop.

## The climate layer: connectivity, resilience, protection

**Rijoly, Leasiwal & Pradana (2026)** — climate-enhanced New Economic Geography, Indonesia-calibrated:

> "This article establishes a climate-enhanced New Economic Geography framework in which climate exposure affects welfare through two interrelated mechanisms: a connectivity channel that raises effective trade and logistics costs, consequently increasing the local price index, and an agglomeration channel that reduces market access and local income potential in peripheral regions."

The archipelagic allocation dilemma, stated directly:

> "how should public resources be distributed among connectivity investment, resilience and social protection to prevent climate risk from entrenching long-run poverty?"

Their calibration shows **combination strategies beat single instruments**: "connectivity investment reduces delivered-cost wedges, resilience lessens the severity and duration of disruptions and adaptive social protection stabilizes welfare during shocks." Poverty risk can rise even when nominal output barely moves, because price differentials raise the cost of basic consumption in remote areas.

## The within-country inequality pattern

**Dwipatna et al. (2026)** — fiscal decentralization's effect splits urban vs. rural:

> "fiscal decentralization is not a neutral policy with respect to inequality. In developed areas, such as urban regions, fiscal decentralization can widen disparities if not accompanied by corrective mechanisms and redistributive policies. Conversely, in underdeveloped areas such as rural regions, fiscal decentralization can be an effective tool for promoting equality when supported by adequate institutional capacity and fiscal governance."

## The Indonesia DAU perverse incentive (education)

**Al-Samarrai & Lewis (2021)** document a second-order effect of the same formula:

> "the formula for the largest general transfers includes incentives for district governments to spend more on hiring civil service teachers than on nonsalary spending. In education, this has resulted in a tendency to hire more teachers than is required to comply with minimum service standards."

Salary spending ≈ 75% of subnational education spending — the formula's incentive structure, not the total envelope, drives the inefficiency.

## Net assessment: what an archipelagic federation should learn from Indonesia

| Observed effect | Causal driver | Design implication for an archipelagic federation |
|---|---|---|
| Mainland bias in transfers | Formula weights land + population, ignores water and dispersion | Cost-of-provision index must include connectivity/dispersion |
| Transfer dependence | Central transfers crowd out own-source revenue effort | Equalization must not reach 100% of spending; reward own effort |
| Special status without results | Papua's 80% revenue share without capacity constraints | Pair asymmetry with capacity-building conditionality |
| Zero-sum equalization | Fixed transfer pool | Grow the pool; or accept explicit mainland compensation |
|Climate poverty trap | Connectivity + price effects | Portfolio approach: connectivity + resilience + adaptive protection |
| Authority-capacity gap | HKPD granted authority before capacity existed | Sequence: capacity first, authority second |
