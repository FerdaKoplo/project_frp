### 1. Simandjuntak (2018), ISEAS Perspective 2018(10) — Indonesia archipelagic provinces / DAU formula
- **Executive summary**: "The bill's most important aspect is a change in the formula of the general-purpose grant (DAU), or the central government's main fund transfer to the regions, aimed at reducing fiscal inequalities among the regions. The current DAU formula only considers variables such as the size of land areas and of the population. With this formula, archipelagic provinces have been receiving less transfers than terrestrial provinces. The new bill seeks to include the size of provincial waters in the formula in order to increase the DAU for archipelagic provinces."
- **Executive summary**: "However, increasing DAU based on territorial waters will not reduce fiscal inequalities across regions, as the increase of transfers to archipelagic regions will decrease those to non-archipelagic regions. Moreover, increasing central transfers will also perpetuate the dependence of the regions on central government resources."
- **Sec. "Archipelagic" definition**: "Archipelagic, or aquatic-terrestrial, provinces are those with territorial waters comprising more than 75% of their total size. In concrete terms, these are Kepri (96%), Maluku (93%), North Maluku (91%), East Nusa Tenggara (88%), West Nusa Tenggara (85%), North Sulawesi (79%), Bangka-Belitung (76%) and Southeast Sulawesi (72%). Some of these are among the poorest in the country."
- **Sec. on DAU derivation**: "The fact that the DAU formula depends on the size of population and the size of land area, and not provincial waters, means that archipelagic regions tend to receive a smaller DAU than land-based regions."
- **Sec. "Towards an asymmetrical decentralization"**: "Indonesia has enacted asymmetrical decentralization in Jakarta, Yogyakarta, Papua (and West Papua) and Aceh. ... Papua, for example, receives 80% of the revenues from its forestry, fishery and mining sectors, and 70% of its oil and gas income until 2026, and 50% thereafter. ... However, the special treatment has not significantly improved local welfare. Papua's poverty rate since 2014 stays stagnant at around 28%, the highest in Indonesia."
- **Sec. "Taking waters into consideration"**: "a simulation study done in 2012 on the DAUs of archipelagic provinces using 2011 data revealed that, although the increase of provincial waters' weightage from 30% to 100% would boost their DAU, it would not significantly raise provincial economic growth. The simulation also showed that an increase of more than 80% would not result in equitable financial capacity, as the increase of DAU of archipelagic regions would reduce those of non-archipelagic provinces."

### 2. Anisyah et al. (2026), Jurnal Interprof 12(2) — archipelagic fiscal constraints under HKPD
- **Abstract (Results/Conclusions)**: "Findings reveal considerable disparities in regional fiscal capacity: provinces with stronger own-source revenue show higher fiscal autonomy and lower transfer dependency. ... The study concludes that fiscal decentralization continues to produce unequal fiscal capacity among archipelagic provinces, supporting Fiscal Federalism Theory by showing fiscal autonomy and transfer dependency as complementary dimensions, with transfers functioning as equalization mechanisms and geographical context shaping these disparities."
- **Introduction**: "Compared with mainland regions, archipelagic provinces face distinctive fiscal challenges arising from dispersed populations, fragmented territories, higher transportation and logistics costs, uneven economic agglomeration, and higher public service delivery costs. These structural characteristics may constrain local governments' ability to generate own-source revenues while simultaneously increasing expenditure needs."
- **Literature review**: "The scattered distribution of islands, limited connectivity, high transportation costs, and the need to provide public services across fragmented territories result in relatively higher administrative costs. On the other hand, limitations in economic agglomeration and the local tax base can constrain local governments' ability to increase their Local Original Revenue (PAD)."
- **Results**: "The geographical condition of a scattered archipelago results in higher public service costs, while economic activity is relatively concentrated in certain areas, thereby further limiting local fiscal space. Thus, the increase in fiscal authority granted through the HKPD Law has not automatically led to an increase in fiscal capacity if it is not supported by regional economic growth and the optimization of local revenue potential."
- **Conclusion**: "Provinces with relatively stronger own-source revenue capacity demonstrate higher fiscal autonomy, whereas provinces with more limited revenue-generating capacity continue to rely more heavily on intergovernmental transfers."

### 3. Said et al. (2020), AABFJ — archipelagic budget trade-offs
- **Abstract**: "Local government budgets behave in trade-offs, between the mainland and the islands area negating each other in their allocations. Affirmations in one area result in weaker allocations in other areas. The dilemma is that local governments are committed to eliminating regional disparities but are not supported by adequate financial capacity. Low financial capacity causes a monopolistic pattern. If an entity leads, then the entity will easier to continue to lead and improve its performance, while other entities remain underdeveloped."
- **Introduction**: "The archipelagic area is often left behind from the mainland in terms of development conducted by the local government. It happens because generally, the mainland is the center of government and the economy; therefore, the distribution of resources tends to accumulate in the mainland."
- **Findings**: "The archetype was built by several subsystems, namely the budget allocation subsystem for the mainland, and the budget allocation subsystem for the archipelagic area. The budget allocation structure explains that the budget allocation made by the Sumenep Regency Government is influenced by several elements including: budget capacity, budget allocation, and development quality."

### 4. Rijoly, Leasiwal & Pradana (2026), Cogent Economics and Finance — climate + archipelagic allocation
- **Abstract**: "This article establishes a climate-enhanced New Economic Geography framework in which climate exposure affects welfare through two interrelated mechanisms: a connectivity channel that raises effective trade and logistics costs, consequently increasing the local price index, and an agglomeration channel that reduces market access and local income potential in peripheral regions. The model highlights a fiscal policy dilemma within a limited budgetary context: subnational governments must allocate scarce resources among connectivity investments, resilience expenditures and social protection initiatives."
- **Introduction**: "For archipelagic nations, this poses a critical policy dilemma: how should public resources be distributed among connectivity investment, resilience and social protection to prevent climate risk from entrenching long-run poverty?"
- **Introduction**: "Indonesia is an appropriate illustrative setting for the model for two related reasons. First, it is the world's largest archipelagic state, with more than 17,000 islands, so remoteness from the national economic core is a structural feature of many outer-island regions."
- **Policy section**: "If climate exposure simultaneously reduces local income potential and raises local living costs, a single policy instrument is unlikely to be sufficient. A combination is required. Connectivity investment mitigates baseline isolation and dampens price pass-through from disruptions. Resilience expenditure protects essential coastal assets ... Adaptive social protection stabilizes welfare when disruptions temporarily raise prices and reduce earnings."

### 7. Haque et al. (2026), World Bank — Pakistan fiscal federalism failure modes
- **Overview**: "First, expenditure assignments remain incompletely implemented and inadequately defined in some areas. The 18th Constitutional Amendment devolved responsibility for social services and economic functions to provinces. However, the federal government continues to operate in constitutionally devolved areas, causing waste and blurring accountability, while local governments lack clearly defined or adequately resourced functional mandates."
- **Overview**: "the 18th Constitutional Amendment led to fragmentation of the tax system. While the Amendment strengthened provincial tax authority, particularly over General Sales Tax (GST) on services, it also split the tax base between five competing jurisdictions. The resulting complexity imposes high compliance costs, discourages interprovincial trade, and has constrained aggregate revenue performance."
- **Overview**: "The National Finance Commission (NFC)-based federal–provincial transfer system provides predictability and protects provincial revenue shares. However, financing has not followed function. The current framework reduced federal resources without a commensurate adjustment in expenditure responsibilities, driving a structural federal fiscal deficit. The horizontal distribution formula also does not achieve genuine fiscal equalization. Finally, this formula provides no meaningful incentives for provincial revenue effort or service delivery performance."
- **Box 11 (equalization design)**: "The design of a fiscal equalization program for Pakistan must be simple, objective, transparent, easy to understand, and based on publicly available data verified by federal and provincial authorities. Any official or private citizen must be able to easily duplicate calculations done by the NFC Secretariat. The size of the equalization pool may be determined exogenously to stay within the ambit of the Constitution ... The proposed approach would require that the fiscal equalization program has two components: fiscal capacity equalization and fiscal need equalization."

### 8. Al-Samarrai & Lewis (eds.) (2021), World Bank — transfer design in practice
- **Ch. 3 (Indonesia DAU)**: "The allocation formula for these transfers is designed to equalize funding among subnational units but not among individuals. This results in districts with large populations receiving lower per capita transfers than districts with smaller populations. Because population size is a critical driver of education costs, these types of allocation rules lead to significant inequalities in education funding."
- **Ch. 3 (cost adjustment)**: "Very few included adjustments in their allocation formulas to address differences in the costs of education provision. Costs diverge among regions because the prices of key inputs vary (for example, the cost of delivering textbooks to remote mountainous regions is higher than that for urban schools) ... Poorer municipalities often incur high costs in providing education services and are disadvantaged further if transfer mechanisms fail to take this into account."
- **Ch. 3 (perverse incentives)**: "In Indonesia, the formula for the largest general transfers includes incentives for district governments to spend more on hiring civil service teachers than on nonsalary spending. In education, this has resulted in a tendency to hire more teachers than is required to comply with minimum service standards and maximum class sizes. Because salary spending accounts for approximately three-quarters of subnational education spending, the perverse incentives included in the transfer's allocation formula is a major driver of this inefficiency."
- **Ch. 4 (Sudan)**: "The horizontal allocation formula that is currently used by the FFAMC at times contradicts the objective of redressing funding imbalances across states. ... states that are able to generate more own-revenue and those that are able to carry a budget surplus are favored in the allocation formula, which defeats the underlying objective of achieving greater equity across states."
- **Ch. 4 (Sudan, transparency)**: "There is little to no evidence that this formula is being applied. The complexity of the formula has made it difficult for the federal government to clearly communicate the basis on which it decides the amount of federal resources to be transferred to the states, which creates a lack of transparency and prevents the achievement of the intended equalization objective."

### 9. Buchanan (1952) — grants and resource allocation
- **Opening (framing via A.D. Scott)**: "There is a valid objection, on the grounds of inefficiency, to making such transfers. This objection is that such transfers provide amenities to poor people in resource-poor states, a situation which may be undesirable in the long run for the following reason: the maximum income for the whole country ... are to be achieved only by maximising national production. This in turn can be achieved only when resources and labour are combined in such a way that the marginal product of similar units of labour is the same in all places."
- **Conclusion section**: "Geographical differences in rewards to like units of resource will be completely eliminated by the free play of economic forces (i.e., no restrictions upon either migration or trade). Factor-price equalization, however, carries with it no full equalization of incomes, although it tends, of course, to reduce the differentials."

### 10. Bucovetsky (1998) — why federations form and equalize
- **Abstract**: "Federations usually allow for considerable freedom of movement among constituent regions. They also provide a fairly limited redistribution of any rents earned in these regions. These two features together imply that residents of poor regions can share in the rents in rich regions by migration. This fiscal migration may waste resources. Why not simply share the rents equally among residents of all regions? Such a unitary arrangement, although efficient, would not appeal to residents of a country which is likely to be the best-off region in a federation."
- **Abstract**: "federation may be chosen by a group of countries, even though it is a more wasteful arrangement than either other alternative, if asymmetries among countries, and the degree of risk aversion, are large. Federal states also often implement transfers among the regions. These transfers can be viewed as substitutes for fiscal migration."
- **Introduction**: "Given the potential problems such migration might cause, why choose such an arrangement? The answer given here is 'risk aversion'. Specifically, what will be required for federalism to be chosen in the model developed here are: (i) uncertainty about resource rents (ii) differences among regions (ex ante) in their expected resource rents (iii) risk aversion."

### 11. Seidel & Schrooten (2000), DIW DP 220 — equalization vs. convergence
- **Abstract**: "In Western economies, the concrete design of fiscal federalism is determined by the status the federation grants to regionally comparable economic and social conditions, and to what extent this status is anchored in the constitution. If the political and social acceptance of regional differences is high, the significance of regional equalisation is low and vice versa. In the case of great regional differences fiscal equalisation could be overburdened by the task of reducing fiscal strength."
- **Abstract**: "international experience makes it clear that strong and well-designed institutional arrangements between the different governmental levels are an important precondition for a high-performing fiscal system. In other words, to take advantage of the potential efficiency gains offered by fiscal federalism, the vertical co-ordination of revenue and expenditure responsibilities between the different governmental levels should be clarified. Problems of destruction and enforcement should be eliminated and replaced by harmonization and co-operation."

### 12. Moges (2003) — Ethiopia failure mode
- **Abstract**: "when fiscal decentralization is exercised with high horizontal and vertical imbalances, it fails to diversify public output in line with the preferences and priorities of local population and to internalize the decisions of regional governments within their own jurisdictions. This in turn encourages the prevalence of big and yet weak government that extracts resources and fails to allocate for the purpose of sustainable and shared economic growth."
- **Concluding remarks**: "The fiscal system is characterized by both vertical and horizontal imbalances that require further decentralization of revenue sources that commensurate the expenditure responsibilities of the regional governments."
- **Concluding remarks**: "Given the prevailing vertical imbalance, the system has not internalized the cost of their expenditure decisions by regions. This in turn tends to give incentive to expansionary fiscal policy stance and bigger government that might not necessarily translate into strong and effective government."

### 13. Arends (2020) — dangers of fiscal decentralization
- **Abstract/Conclusion**: "The overall conclusion of this review is that the dangers of decentralization are highly relevant to local public services in terms of the efficiency, equality, and accountability of service provision. Although there is evidence to support both the decentralization-enthusiastic and the decentralization-skeptical views, there are means of mitigating risks: When decentralizing public services, reformers should know the specificities of the respective public service, the local context, and the effects of the design of fiscal relations like the backs of their hands. If things go wrong, recentralization is a viable option."
- **Theory section**: "While the first generation assumes the public servant is a welfare maximizer, the second generation argues that public servants at the local level may have other interests than promoting the public good, potentially compromising the beneficial effects of decentralization."
- **Theory section**: "Prud'homme (1995) groups the dangers of decentralization according to Musgrave's three objectives of public financial and fiscal policy: the negative effects on macroeconomic stability, possible detrimental effects on efficiency in service delivery, and the risk of increasing regional disparities in income. Furthermore, discussion of these dangers of decentralization puts the spotlight on a previously underreported additional danger, namely the danger of local corruption and collusion."

### 14. Chaitkin (2022) — Nigeria fragmentation
- **Health financing landscape**: "The 1999 Constitution specifies extensive exclusive and concurrent powers for the federal and state legislatures. In contrast, the functions of local government councils are modest and many fall under state government leadership ... Moreover, the federal government raised more than 92% of government revenue in 2019 ... but much of this is transferred. In 2016, state and local governments accounted for 36% and 11% of government spending, respectively."
- **Health financing landscape**: "Despite local government rights to revenue, there is a history of states eroding the local tier's fiscal autonomy. Tactics have included diverting federal revenue intended for LGAs to state-defined priorities and operating parallel revenue boards."
- **PFM section**: "There is an extensive PFM regime at the federal level, while state systems are much less developed."
- **Key findings**: "Health reforms are slow to overcome the severe fragmentation throughout the health budget cycle. States jealously guard their fiscal autonomy and their planning, management, and implementation of government spending is largely disconnected from corresponding processes at the federal level. There is minimal coordination or information sharing across levels of government, and the federal tier cannot legally impose many mechanisms or standards for reporting, oversight, or accountability on the sub-national levels."

### 15. Allain-Dupré (2020) — asymmetric/differentiated governance
- **Abstract**: "the way multi-level governance systems are designed has a direct impact on policy outcomes, hence the imperative to strengthen multi-level governance systems."
- **On differentiation**: "The current rationale for differentiated subnational governance goes well beyond identity issues, to reflect the capacity and efficiency dimensions. ... Accommodating diverse preferences for political and fiscal autonomy across regions may help maintain political stability. The benefits of differentiated subnational governance are increasingly linked to the fact that institutional and fiscal frameworks are better targeted to local capacities, and may allow a better response to local needs. In general, asymmetric decentralisation favours experimentation, learning-by-doing and innovation in policy-making."
- **On coordination costs**: "Formal co-ordination and collaboration procedures may imply some transaction costs, at least in the short term. Policy makers need to balance these costs with the long-term benefits brought about by consistent and regular cooperation. These costs tend to rise exponentially as the number of jurisdictions rises. Different mechanisms and background conditions, such as clarity in the assignment and objectives, trust, the establishment of adequate incentives, contractual arrangements, arbitration mechanisms, can help limit the costs and maximise the benefits of coordination."

### 16. Simonov (n.d.) — differentiated regional policy architecture
- **Conclusion**: "The proposed three-level architecture combines a common federal accountability core, profile-specific modules, and project-level adaptive coordination. Its central principle is that policy unity should rest on common rules of justification, measurement, and review, whereas instruments and additional indicators should reflect the diagnosed regional problem. Under these conditions, differentiation is not a concession to fragmentation. It is a mechanism for substantive equality, institutional learning, and politically accountable federal governance."
- **Introduction**: "Territorial inequality has a double political meaning. It concerns the distribution of income, infrastructure, and economic opportunity, but it also changes the environment in which public authorities formulate and implement collective decisions. ... A formally uniform policy instrument may create dissimilar incentives and may distribute benefits and costs among different constituencies. Regional heterogeneity is therefore not merely an external condition to be corrected after a policy is designed; it is part of the policy problem itself."

### 17. Dwipatna et al. (2026) — place-based decentralization (Indonesia)
- **Policy implications**: "fiscal decentralization is not a neutral policy with respect to inequality. In developed areas, such as urban regions, fiscal decentralization can widen disparities if not accompanied by corrective mechanisms and redistributive policies. Conversely, in underdeveloped areas such as rural regions, fiscal decentralization can be an effective tool for promoting equality when supported by adequate institutional capacity and fiscal governance."
- **Policy implications**: "fiscal decentralization should be designed using a place-based approach, considering that its impact on income inequality varies significantly between urban and rural areas. ... Therefore, strengthening redistributive fiscal transfer mechanisms and sharpening regional spending priorities are necessary so that fiscal decentralization does not deepen income gaps."

### The three biggest evidence gaps
1. **Non-Indonesian archipelagic federations.** No Philippines, Solomon Islands, Comoros, Micronesia, or Fiji case material. The "archipelagic nation + federal system" pairing rests almost entirely on Indonesia, which is formally unitary (with asymmetrical features), not federal. This is the single most important collection gap for the paper's framing.
2. **"Resource planning" as a concept and as optimization.** No source treats resource planning as a formal optimization problem (objective, constraints, instruments across tiers). The closest are the NEG fiscal-allocation model in Rijoly et al. (2026) and the equalization formulas. A methodological bridging source (operations research / planning theory applied to intergovernmental finance) is absent.
3. **Political constraints → algorithmic rules.** Only formula-case evidence (Pakistan Box 11, DAU weights, Sudan criteria, Philippines IRA). No general treatment of how bargaining, veto players, and elite capture get encoded in or excluded from transfer formulas. Karapin (2020) documents veto-player dynamics in the US but for energy, not fiscal formulas.
