# Chance of Economic Development With Federation Resources Planning

> Source basis: Loonena Naisho 2024; Amagoh & Amin 2012; Dwipatna et al. 2026; Huseynova 2026; Karapin 2020; IJRAR19D5942 2017; Said et al. 2020. See [[corpus_profile]].

**The honest answer: the evidence is split.** Federation-level resource planning is not a growth policy — it is an institutional arrangement whose growth effect depends on conditions. This note collects the evidence on both directions.

## Evidence FOR: the mechanisms that could raise development

**Loonena Naisho (2024), US states** — identifies three pro-growth mechanisms:

> "the study identifies three primary mechanisms through which fiscal autonomy may impact economic development: policy experimentation, resource allocation efficiency, and responsiveness to local economic conditions."

Fiscal autonomy lets jurisdictions experiment, match spending to local needs, and respond faster. In a large developed federation, these channels measurably contribute.

**Dwipatna et al. (2026), Indonesia** — the effect is place-dependent:

> "in underdeveloped areas such as rural regions, fiscal decentralization can be an effective tool for promoting equality when supported by adequate institutional capacity and fiscal governance."

**IJRAR19D5942 (2017)** — the conceptual case: subsidiarity, revenue sharing, and equalization jointly align spending with regional development priorities.

## Evidence AGAINST: when the same mechanisms fail

**Amagoh & Amin (2012)** — the compression of all caveats into one sentence:

> "while there are many benefits to be gained from fiscal decentralization, its impacts on economic growth is constrained by a number of factors that are based on the contexts of the societies/economies involved. Hence the positive impact of fiscal decentralization on growth depends on the context and the society/economy."

**Moges (2003), Ethiopia** — when imbalances are high, decentralization extracts without allocating: government becomes "big and yet weak... extracts resources and fails to allocate for the purpose of sustainable and shared economic growth."

**Said et al. (2020), Indonesia** — at the archipelagic local level, budget trade-offs entrench the pattern: "Low financial capacity causes a monopolistic pattern. If an entity leads, then the entity will easier to continue to lead" — the "success to the successful" archetype. Left-behind regions do not catch up; leading regions keep leading.

## The decisive intervening variable: institutions

**Huseynova (2026)** states the synthesis:

> "fiscal decentralization enhances allocative responsiveness and policy flexibility but may intensify regional disparities when equalization mechanisms are weak. Conversely, centralized coordination strengthens macro-fiscal stability and strategic policy alignment but does not automatically ensure spatial convergence. The findings suggest that allocation efficiency depends primarily on institutional alignment between fiscal architecture, administrative capacity, and governance coordination."

Translation: neither decentralization nor centralization delivers development on its own. **Institutional alignment** — fiscal architecture × administrative capacity × coordination — is the operative variable.

**Seidel & Schrooten (2000)** make the same point institutionally:

> "to take advantage of the potential efficiency gains offered by fiscal federalism, the vertical co-ordination of revenue and expenditure responsibilities between the different governmental levels should be clarified."

## Policy skepticism from an unexpected angle

**Karapin (2020), US energy transition** — federalism is a double-edged sword even in a rich federation:

> "state governments have taken the initiative in renewable-energy policy in every period, but the role of the federal government has depended strongly on the president's political commitments... Increased party polarization over the last two decades has sharpened this pattern."

State-level initiative is real, but its aggregate value depends on federal commitment — which is political, not institutional. Multi-level arrangements amplify whichever political direction holds.

## What this means for an archipelagic adopter

The development chance is **conditional**. Conditions for the positive branch:

1. **Sequence before granting authority** — revenue administration and service-delivery capacity first (Moges, Anisyah)
2. **Equalization mechanisms must be strong** — or regional disparities widen (Huseynova, Dwipatna)
3. **Institutional alignment across tiers** — fiscal architecture matched with administrative capacity (Huseynova)
4. **Place-based design** — uniform rules have heterogeneous effects (Dwipatna)
5. **Stable federal commitment** — subnational initiative needs federal consistency (Karapin)

Without those conditions, the archipelagic federation inherits the pattern in Said et al.: leading islands pull ahead, peripheral islands stagnate, and equalization transfers fund the gap without closing it.
