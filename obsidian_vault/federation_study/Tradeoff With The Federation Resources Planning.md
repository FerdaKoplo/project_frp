# Tradeoff With The Federation Resources Planning

> Source basis: Said et al. 2020; Rijoly et al. 2026; Buchanan 1952; Bucovetsky 1998; Ter-Minassian 1997; Seidel & Schrooten 2000; Allain-Dupré 2020. See [[corpus_profile]].

Federal resource planning is a **system of mutually exclusive goods**. Every mechanism that solves one problem creates another. Optimization means choosing positions on five named trade-off axes — not eliminating tension.

## Trade-off 1: Mainland vs. Islands (the archipelagic core)

**Said et al. (2020)** — the budget behaves as a zero-sum system:

> "Local government budgets behave in trade-offs, between the mainland and the islands area negating each other in their allocations. Affirmations in one area result in weaker allocations in other areas."

Every rupiah of affirmative allocation to islands comes out of mainland allocations — within a fixed local budget. Political resistance is structural, not ideological.

## Trade-off 2: Connectivity vs. Resilience vs. Social Protection

**Rijoly, Leasiwal & Pradana (2026)** — the climate-era allocation problem:

> "subnational governments must allocate scarce resources among connectivity investments, resilience expenditures and social protection initiatives."

Calibration result: "combinations of policy instruments can outperform single-instrument strategies." No single instrument dominates; the optimum is a **portfolio**, and its composition shifts with exposure level.

## Trade-off 3: Equity vs. Efficiency (the classical axis)

**Buchanan (1952)** — equalization transfers have an efficiency cost. Transfers move resources away from their highest marginal product:

> "the maximum income for the whole country ... are to be achieved only by maximising national production. This in turn can be achieved only when resources and labour are combined in such a way that the marginal product of similar units of labour is the same in all places."

But markets don't finish the job: "Factor-price equalization ... carries with it no full equalization of incomes." So the planner cannot escape the trade-off — only position on it.

## Trade-off 4: Autonomy vs. Equalization

**Bucovetsky (1998)** — migration vs. transfers as substitute redistribution mechanisms:

> "Federal states also often implement transfers among the regions. These transfers can be viewed as substitutes for fiscal migration."

Redistribution **through** migration preserves efficiency; redistribution **through** transfers preserves settlement patterns but dulls local effort. A federation chooses a mix.

**Ter-Minassian (1997)** — the practical version: arrangements assigning all taxing power to the center "obscure the link between the benefits of public expenditures and their price" and kill fiscal responsibility; arrangements assigning everything subnationally lose macroeconomic control and invite tax competition eroding the base.

## Trade-off 5: Simplicity vs. Need-Sensitivity

Equalization formula design trade-off, from Ter-Minassian's four-country comparison:
- **Australia** — full equalization of capacity + standard: most just, "but it is also very complex, and demanding in terms of information requirements"
- **Canada** — taxing capacity only: simple, blind to expenditure needs
- **Denmark/Japan/Korea** — simple need indicators (population density, road length): cheap, coarse
- **Germany** — VAT per capita: transparent, weak redistribution

**Hutchinson & LaFond (2004)**: the Philippine IRA (50% population, 25% land area, 25% category) is simple "but may bear little relation to differences in need or in the costs of service provision." Simple formulas ignore cost dispersion — precisely the archipelagic penalty.

## Trade-off 6: Uniformity vs. Asymmetry

**Allain-Dupré (2020)**:

> "Accommodating diverse preferences for political and fiscal autonomy across regions may help maintain political stability. The benefits of differentiated subnational governance are increasingly linked to the fact that institutional and fiscal frameworks are better targeted to local capacities... In general, asymmetric decentralisation favours experimentation, learning-by-doing and innovation in policy-making."

But asymmetry has a cost — coordination:

> "Formal co-ordination and collaboration procedures may imply some transaction costs... These costs tend to rise exponentially as the number of jurisdictions rises."

An archipelago of many small islands is the **worst case** for coordination-cost scaling.

## Trade-off 7: Equalization Ambition vs. Political Feasibility

**Seidel & Schrooten (2000)**:

> "If the political and social acceptance of regional differences is high, the significance of regional equalisation is low and vice versa. In the case of great regional differences fiscal equalisation could be overburdened by reducing fiscal strength."

Transfer ambition must fit the federation's tolerance for redistribution — an over-ambitious formula provokes the exact backlash that dismantles it.

## The optimization frontier

The seven axes are not independent. Two couplings dominate:

1. **Equity-efficiency × autonomy-equalization** — moves to equalize reduce autonomy returns; reducing equalization increases migration pressure (Buchanan/Bucovetsky).
2. **Simplicity-need × uniformity-asymmetry** — need-sensitive formulas push toward asymmetric arrangements; uniform formulas hide cost differences (Ter-Minassian/Allain-Dupré).

Consequence: an archipelagic federation cannot optimize each axis separately. Optimization is **joint**, over the formula vector (weights, base, conditionalities, asymmetric tiers) subject to political constraints.
